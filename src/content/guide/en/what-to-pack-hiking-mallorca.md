Mallorca reads as a beach island, and people pack accordingly. The Serra de Tramuntana is a limestone mountain range with 1,400 m summits, very little shade, sharp rock, and almost no water. The gap between those two mental models is where most problems start.

Here is what actually matters.

## The short list

For a normal day route:

- **2 litres of water** per person, minimum. Three in summer.
- **Footwear with real grip** — boots or proper trail shoes
- **Sun hat, sunglasses, high-factor sunscreen**
- **A windproof layer**, even in June
- **Food**, including something salty
- **A small first-aid kit** with blister plasters
- **Offline map** on your phone, and a power bank

That is it. A 20–25 litre pack covers it comfortably.

## Water, in more detail

This is the single most important item and the one people get wrong.

There is **no reliable drinking water on Tramuntana routes**. Some springs run seasonally and some do not run at all. The reservoirs at Cúber and Gorg Blau are not drinkable. A few summit sanctuaries have a tap, but they close on weekdays out of season.

Two litres per person is the working minimum for a half day. In summer we tell groups three, and we mean it. Dehydration, not difficulty, is what turns Mallorcan walks into incidents.

## Footwear

The rock is the reason. Tramuntana limestone comes in two unhelpful forms:

- **Karst** — fissured, sharp-edged pavement on the high ground, which will destroy soft soles and turn ankles
- **Polished stone** — the worn cobbles of the old mule tracks and the water-smoothed rock in the gorges, which is genuinely slippery, especially when dusty

Both want a stiff sole with a decent tread pattern. Ankle support matters more on descents than climbs.

**Do not bring new boots.** The Barranc de Biniaraix has ended more than one holiday's walking on the first day for exactly this reason.

## Layers

The temptation is to pack for the temperature at sea level. Two things change that:

**Altitude.** Expect roughly 6 °C cooler at 1,000 m than at the coast.

**The tramuntana wind.** The north wind the range is named for arrives with little warning, and on an exposed ridge it turns a warm day cold in minutes. A light windproof shell weighs almost nothing and is the layer we most often see people wishing they had.

For most of the season the working system is a base layer, a light fleece or midlayer in the pack, and a windproof shell. Add a waterproof from October to March.

## Sun

Mallorca sits at 39° north with a Mediterranean sun and very little tree cover above the treeline. Sun hat, sunglasses and factor 50 are not optional between March and October — and the days people get burnt are the cool, windy ones where they do not notice it happening.

## Food

Bring more than you think, and include salt. On long hot days, plain sugar is not enough — salted nuts, olives, or a sandwich with something cured in it does more for you at hour five.

There is rarely anywhere to buy food on route. A few sanctuaries and beach restaurants exist, all with seasonal and unpredictable hours.

## Navigation

Marked routes in the Tramuntana are variable. The **GR221** is generally well signed; many other paths are not, and old cairns lead in unhelpful directions.

Download offline maps before you go — mobile coverage disappears in gorges and on north-facing slopes. Carry a power bank; cold and constant screen use flatten a phone faster than expected.

## What we bring

On a guided day we carry a first-aid kit, communication equipment, a rope where the route warrants one, and spare water. You still need your own water, footwear and sun protection — but you do not need to think about the rest of it, and we will tell you honestly before the day if something in your kit will not do.
