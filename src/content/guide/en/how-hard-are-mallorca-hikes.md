"Is it hard?" is the question we get most, and the honest answer is that it depends on things the numbers do not show.

## Why Mallorca feels harder than the distance suggests

Take a 10 km walk with 600 m of ascent. In the Lake District or the Black Forest, that is a pleasant half day. Here, the same numbers are consistently harder, for four reasons:

**The ground.** Tramuntana limestone is either sharp fissured karst that has to be picked across, or centuries-old cobbled steps of uneven height. Neither lets you settle into a rhythm the way a dirt path does. Speed drops and the legs work differently.

**The heat.** From May to October, the temperature does most of the damage. A climb that is comfortable at 15 °C is a different proposition at 28 °C with no shade.

**No water.** You carry everything. Two or three litres is two or three kilos, and it is on your back for the whole climb.

**The descents.** People plan for the up and are undone by the down. Long flights of stone steps — the Barranc de Biniaraix is the extreme case — are punishing on knees and quads, and the descent often takes almost as long as the ascent.

## How we grade routes

Every route page on this site uses one of four grades. They describe the whole day, not the hardest ten minutes of it.

### Easy

Up to about 4 hours and 400 m of ascent, on good paths, no exposure, no scrambling. Suitable for anyone who walks regularly, and for children of roughly eight and up.

*Example: Deià to Port de Sóller.*

### Moderate

Four to six hours and 400–700 m of ascent. Sustained uphill sections and rougher ground, but no technical difficulty. You should be comfortable walking uphill for an hour or more at a time.

*Examples: Castell d'Alaró, Puig de l'Ofre, Camí de s'Arxiduc.*

### Challenging

Five to seven hours and 700–1,000 m of ascent, often on rough or awkward ground, sometimes with easy scrambling or mild exposure. A full day that requires real fitness and leaves you tired.

*Examples: Barranc de Biniaraix, Puig de Massanella.*

### Demanding

Long days, technical ground, sustained scrambling, exposure, or serious weather dependence. These are for experienced walkers who want exactly this.

*Example: Torrent de Pareis.*

## Reading the numbers properly

**Cumulative ascent tells you more than distance.** It is the single best predictor of how a day will feel. Anything above 800 m is a serious day whatever the kilometres say.

**Walking time is moving time.** It excludes stops. A route listed at 5–6 hours is a 7–8 hour day once you have eaten, looked at things and taken photographs. Plan the day around the second figure.

**Exposure is separate from difficulty.** The Camí de s'Arxiduc is only moderate physically, but a long section runs beside an unprotected cliff edge. If heights are an issue for you, that matters more than the ascent figure — and it is worth saying so when you get in touch.

## Being honest with yourself

The most useful thing you can tell us is not a fitness self-assessment but a fact: *what was the last long walk you did, and how did it feel?*

"We did 15 km in the Peak District in April and were fine but tired" tells us exactly what to plan. "We're pretty fit" tells us nothing, and it is the sentence that most often precedes a hard afternoon.

Turning back is normal. We would much rather grade a day slightly easy and have everyone enjoy it than pitch it right at the ceiling and spend the last two hours grinding.
