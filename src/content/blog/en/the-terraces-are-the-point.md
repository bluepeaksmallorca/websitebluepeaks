Almost every visitor arrives thinking the Serra de Tramuntana is protected for the view. It is a reasonable assumption. It is also wrong, and the correction is the most interesting thing we can offer on a walking day.

The range was inscribed by UNESCO in 2011 as a **cultural landscape**. What is protected is not the limestone. It is what people did to it.

## What you are actually walking through

Look up from the path on the climb out of Biniaraix. The slope above you is not a hillside — it is a staircase of stone-walled terraces, running up several hundred metres, each one holding a strip of soil that was carried there.

There are thousands of kilometres of these walls in the Tramuntana. All built without mortar, stone fitted to stone, by hand, over roughly a thousand years, beginning under Islamic rule and continuing well into the twentieth century.

They exist because the range is too steep to farm and the rain arrives all at once. A terrace does three things: it holds soil that would otherwise wash to the sea, it slows the water, and it makes a flat surface that a person and a mule can work on.

## The water is the clever part

The terraces are the visible half. The system that makes them work is the water.

Springs high in the range were captured and led into **channels** — *síquies* — that contour along the hillsides at a gradient just steep enough to move water and gentle enough not to erode the channel. Some of them run for kilometres. They feed cisterns, mills, and the irrigated gardens around the villages.

A lot of it still works. In Biniaraix you can stand beside a channel carrying water that was captured somewhere above you and has been arriving in this village, by this route, for several hundred years. Nobody switched it on this morning. It just does not stop.

## The other structures

Once you start looking, the range fills up with things that had a purpose:

**Sitges** — circular flat platforms in the woods, often mistaken for viewpoints. They are charcoal-burning floors. Woodcutters stacked holm oak on them, covered it with earth, and burned it slowly for a week or more.

**Cases de neu** — stone-lined snow pits high on Massanella and elsewhere. Snow was packed in winter, insulated, and carried down to Palma in summer. There was a professional trade in it.

**Forns de calç** — lime kilns, for turning limestone into the lime used in building and whitewash.

**The mule tracks themselves** — the cobbled paths you are walking on are engineering. The step heights are consistent because they were built for a loaded animal, not for you.

## Why it matters on a walk

Because it changes the register of the day.

Without it, the Barranc de Biniaraix is two thousand steps and a good view — a stair-climbing exercise in nice surroundings. With it, you are walking up through a working machine that a very large number of people built by hand, for a reason, and much of which is still doing its job.

The craft is not dead, either. **Dry-stone walling** — *pedra en sec* — is a living trade in Mallorca with a formal school and a steady stream of restoration work, and UNESCO added the technique itself to its intangible heritage list in 2018.

That is what we mean when we say the walking here rewards knowing what you are looking at. The view is free. The rest of it takes someone pointing at a wall and telling you why it is that shape.
